The text examines how Nicolae Ceaușescu’s communist regime in Romania used Decree 770, introduced in 1966, to severely restrict abortion and control women’s reproduction. The policy was intended to increase Romania’s birth rate and was enforced through an extensive system involving police, prosecutors, the Securitate, medical institutions, and informants.

A particularly tragic example is the case of a 35-year-old woman in Zalău in 1987 who arrived at a hospital suffering a severe miscarriage. Although she urgently needed medical treatment, authorities suspected her of deliberately causing an illegal abortion. Doctors were prevented from treating her while officials tried to force a confession. She was left untreated for five days and died. A later forensic report found no evidence that she had induced the miscarriage.

The text argues that her death illustrates how state repression penetrated hospitals and turned medical care into an instrument of surveillance and punishment. Doctors were required to report suspected abortions, while police and prosecutors investigated women and medical professionals. Informants were recruited inside hospitals, and medical workers could lose their licenses or face imprisonment.

### Main points

Pronatalist policy: Ceaușescu promoted motherhood as a national duty and wanted Romanian women to have more children.

Decree 770: Beginning in 1966, abortion was almost completely prohibited except under limited circumstances.

Medical surveillance: Doctors were required to notify prosecutors about emergency abortions and suspected illegal procedures.

Police and Securitate involvement: Authorities monitored pregnancies, investigated suspected abortions, recruited informants in hospitals, and sometimes interrogated women while they were seriously ill.

Abuse of women: Early attempts to prevent humiliating investigations were abandoned. By the 1970s, authorities explicitly demanded tougher enforcement.

Punishment: Thousands of people were prosecuted for violating abortion restrictions. Between 1967 and 1973, 6,350 people were indicted, including doctors and other healthcare workers.

Public propaganda: The regime used trials, media campaigns, and films such as Postcards with Wildflowers to discourage abortion and create fear around it.

Unintended consequences: As legal abortion became inaccessible and dangerous, more women turned to unqualified practitioners or attempted abortions themselves, resulting in increasingly severe infections and deaths.

Doctors caught in the system: Medical professionals were placed in a conflict between providing emergency care and complying with police/prosecutorial requirements.

Growing surveillance: By 1984, the government ordered surveillance of all medical units, including facilities without obstetrics and gynecology departments and workplaces with predominantly female staff.

Broader responsibility: The text emphasizes that repression depended not only on Ceaușescu himself but on a large network of police, prosecutors, medical authorities, informants, and other officials who enforced the policy.
 
### Central theme

The central argument is that Decree 770 transformed reproductive healthcare into a system of state surveillance and repression. The Zalău woman’s death demonstrates the extreme consequences: suspicion of an illegal abortion could take priority over saving a patient's life—even when the suspected abortion had never occurred.

### 1984 in 1984

Medical surveillance increased: In 1984, Romania’s Internal Affairs Ministry approved a secret plan placing medical facilities and female-heavy workplaces under Securitate surveillance.

Doctors and medical workers were recruited as informants:
In Cluj County, the Securitate reported 72 informants and 13 collaborators, including 54 doctors.
In Timiș County, there were 51 information sources, including doctors, nurses, and pharmacists.

Abortions were increasingly criminalized: The number of people convicted for abortion-related offenses doubled in 1986 compared with 1985. Of 1,316 people convicted in 1986, 26 were doctors and 102 were other health workers.
72% received prison sentences.
Women who performed abortions on themselves generally received correctional work or fines, rather than prison.

Informants actively reported women: Individuals such as Gheorghe Toabeș and Fănică Panait informed the Securitate about women and girls suspected of having or considering abortions. Their reports could lead to police warnings or criminal investigations.

Some informants later entered local government: After the 1989 Revolution, both Toabeș and Panait became local councillors, despite later being exposed by CNSAS as former Securitate collaborators.

Police participated in gynecological examinations: Women and girls were subjected to medical examinations in which police officers were sometimes present. Although officially presented as health checks, their underlying purpose was often to detect undeclared pregnancies and abortions.

The system encouraged widespread denunciation: People sometimes informed on neighbors, colleagues, daughters, wives, or fellow doctors, creating an atmosphere of fear, mistrust, opportunism, and dehumanization.

Doctors and officials sometimes resisted: Some doctors refused to report pregnancies or abortions, and some police officers and prosecutors deliberately ignored violations. However, these examples were described as exceptions.

The consequences could be fatal: The text describes women who became seriously ill or died after unsafe abortions. A major obstacle to accountability after communism was the lack of reliable medical records and the difficulty of proving what happened through testimony alone.

### Bottom line

The passage portrays Romania’s abortion policy not simply as a law banning abortion, but as a broader system of state surveillance and social control. The government mobilized medical institutions, police, the Securitate, and ordinary citizens to monitor women’s reproductive lives, while fear of denunciation and criminal punishment contributed to dangerous abortions and, in some cases, death.
