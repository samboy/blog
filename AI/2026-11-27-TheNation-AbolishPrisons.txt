This article argues why we should abolish prisons.

Imagine the debate unfolding not as a newspaper exchange but as a quiet, urgent conversation among organizers weighing the price of influence. The central tension is not “wokeness” itself, but whether a socialist movement should treat the prison system as a permanent fixture that can only be trimmed or as an institution that must ultimately be dismantled.

The argument begins with scale and principle. The United States locks up roughly two million people—the largest incarcerated population on the planet. Even a substantial reduction would still leave the country as a global outlier, disproportionately caging the poor, the poorly represented, and Black and Latino communities. From this vantage, calls for full abolition are not fringe theatrics; they continue a long socialist tradition that treats the prison-industrial complex as inseparable from the conditions—poverty, racism, desperation—that produce most punished crime. Figures like Angela Davis, Howard Zinn, and Eugene Debs (in his sharper moments) treated the demand to empty the cages as inseparable from the demand for freedom itself. Zinn’s plain assessment is recalled: imprisonment pretends to solve crime while doing nothing for victims and leaving the crimes of the powerful largely untouched.

Only after establishing that baseline does the critique turn to the present tactical proposal labeled “carceral minimalism.” Its advocates contend that some version of policing and incarceration is necessary to protect the vulnerable and that abolition is politically unviable. They therefore urge socialists to accept a permanent, if smaller, carceral system in order to build a broader electoral coalition. The response is that this position never specifies an acceptable number of human beings who must remain caged, and that settling for “fewer” still means accepting a racialized lottery with people’s lives. It is presented as a strategic peace with the very apparatus that has long been used to manage surplus populations.

The pace then slows to examine history as cautionary evidence. Early-twentieth-century American socialism offers a parallel: when the movement treated race as secondary or refused to make special appeals, it both failed to root itself in Black communities and provided cover for its own openly racist right wing. The damage lingered, contributed to splits, and helped clear space for a more uncompromising Communist Party that did prioritize anti-racist organizing. The suggestion that today’s praise for certain historical figures and the simultaneous push for carceral minimalism form a coherent branding effort is left hanging as an observation rather than a conspiracy claim.

Only late in the reckoning does the specific provocation appear: a New York Times op-ed that frames “wokeness” as a political liability, uses a years-old college controversy as illustrative evidence of excess, and ends by urging democratic socialists to stop criticizing those who favor a more law-and-order posture. The op-ed is treated less as the origin of the dispute and more as a symptom—an instance of elite-adjacent language being used to police the boundaries of acceptable left strategy. The deeper risk identified is internal: that adopting this stance may separate the movement from the very multiracial, radical energy that has been swelling its ranks, repeating older patterns of trading anti-oppression principle for the appearance of pragmatism.

The summary closes where the underlying conviction sits: in a moment of genuine possibility, choosing to align with the political establishment’s “war on woke” and to introduce carceral minimalism as a socialist aim does more to undercut organizers working in the movement’s better traditions than it does to clear a path to power. Abolition, in this telling, is not the crazy position; the fantasy is that socialism can be reached by discarding the anti-oppression commitments that once defined its most effective multiracial efforts.
