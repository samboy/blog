Rigid abstinence doctrines still dominate large parts of the recovery landscape, creating unnecessary conflict between two of the most useful tools available: evidence-based medications and peer support. Medications such as methadone and buprenorphine (Suboxone) reduce overdose and death rates by 50 to 80 percent. In clinical settings they are treated much like insulin for diabetes—necessary, non-negotiable supports for a chronic condition. Yet in many 12-step rooms the same medications are treated as evidence that a person is not truly “in recovery.” People hanging on by a thread in early recovery are routinely shamed for using them, even when the drugs are prescribed and monitored.

The same ideological rigidity extends to other substances that can serve harm-reduction purposes. Controlled studies and clinical experience increasingly support the therapeutic potential of psychedelics for alcohol and opioid use disorders. Cannabis, while not risk-free, has helped many patients manage withdrawal and transition away from far more lethal drugs; for some it functions as an exit ramp rather than an entry point. A 2014 JAMA analysis further undercuts the fear of inevitable “drug substitution”: people who achieve remission from one substance-use disorder actually show lower, not higher, risk of developing a new one. The coping skills acquired in recovery appear to generalize.

These findings sit uneasily with an organizational culture whose core assumptions date to 1937, long before modern pharmacology, neuroimaging, or contemporary research methods existed. Alcoholics Anonymous and its offshoots have helped a self-selecting subset of people, and that contribution should not be dismissed. The difficulty arises when an abstinence-only framework developed in a pre-scientific era is treated as the sole legitimate definition of recovery nearly a century later. The result is a narrow tent that excludes many who are using medically supervised treatments or less harmful alternatives to stay alive and functional.

A more coherent approach would expand rather than police the boundaries of recovery. Peer support remains invaluable; so do medications and, for some, carefully considered use of cannabis or psychedelics. Clinicians already meet patients across the full spectrum of socioeconomic and clinical presentations. Recovery communities can do the same—welcoming those on agonist therapy, those exploring newer pharmacological options, and those whose personal definitions of sobriety differ from classical 12-step orthodoxy. When the alternative is stigma that drives people away from both meetings and medication, the stakes are measured in preventable deaths. Expanding the tent is therefore not ideological softness; it is a practical response to the evidence and to the lived reality of people still fighting to stay free of the drugs that once controlled them.
