The text explains the famous Monty Hall problem, a probability puzzle that sparked intense debate among the public and mathematicians after Marilyn vos Savant published the correct answer: you should switch doors.

### Main points
The puzzle: You choose one of three doors. One hides a car and two hide goats. The host, who knows what is behind the doors, opens a different door to reveal a goat and offers you the chance to switch.

The correct strategy under the standard rules: Switching gives you a 2/3 chance of winning, while staying with your original choice gives you only a 1/3 chance.

Why people were confused: Many mathematicians initially argued that after one goat is revealed, the two remaining doors should each have a 1/2 chance. This is incorrect because the host's knowledge and deliberate choice of which door to open matter.

Monty Hall himself demonstrated it: In a small experiment, staying with the original door produced 4 cars in 10 trials, while switching produced 8 cars in 10 trials.

Probability experts admitted the mistake: Some mathematicians eventually acknowledged that they had been wrong. Persi Diaconis explained that humans are generally poor at intuitively reasoning about probability.

There is an important loophole: The original wording did not explicitly require the host to always open a goat-containing door and always offer the contestant a switch.

Why that matters: If the host can choose when to offer the switch, his behavior provides additional information. The usual 2/3 answer only applies when the host always follows the standard procedure.

Monty Hall exploited this loophole: He showed that he could manipulate the contestant by choosing whether to offer a switch and by using money to influence the contestant's decision.

### Final conclusion

If the host must always reveal a goat and offer a switch → switch.

If the host can decide whether to offer the switch → the answer depends on his motives and behavior.

### Bottom line

The famous answer "always switch" is mathematically correct under the standard Monty Hall rules, but the original version of the puzzle was ambiguously worded.
