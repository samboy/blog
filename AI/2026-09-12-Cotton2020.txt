Senator Tom Cotton of Arkansas argues that the federal government should immediately deploy military forces under the Insurrection Act to halt urban disorder. He frames this as fulfilling a constitutional duty to protect states from domestic violence, noting the Act’s long history of use by presidents of both parties—Eisenhower, Kennedy, and Johnson to enforce school desegregation against state resistance, and George H.W. Bush to quell the 1992 Los Angeles riots—without constituting martial law or violating the Posse Comitatus Act.

Only after establishing this legal and historical foundation does the piece turn to the present crisis: widespread looting, arson, and attacks on officers in cities including New York, Las Vegas, and St. Louis, which Cotton describes as opportunistic criminality and left-wing radical infiltration rather than legitimate protest over George Floyd’s death. Floyd’s own family, he notes, has condemned the violence. Local police are outnumbered; some governors have activated the National Guard while others have not, leaving gaps that require federal backup.

Cotton closes by citing polling data showing majority support (including substantial shares of Democrats and African-American voters) for using the military to restore order, and by insisting that government’s core obligation is public safety—something local authorities alone cannot currently guarantee.
