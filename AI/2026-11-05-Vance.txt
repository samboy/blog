This is Vice President JD Vance’s post-mortem on the 2026 midterms. He
argues Republican turnout was solid (Florida’s electorate was still
~9 points more Republican than Democratic, close to 2024 levels), but
many 2024 Trump/Vance voters crossed over to Democrats. The top reasons
those switchers cited, per a pollster he trusts who correctly called
the results, were: end the war, focus on cost of living/inflation, put
America First, and change the U.S. relationship with Israel. He pins
the primary blame on “the war,” notes Trump himself acknowledged
it as a factor, and rejects amnesty as the fix while remaining open to
enforcement changes targeting employers.

### Context from the results

Democrats flipped the House (gains of ~30+ seats) and Senate (net +7 or
more, reaching at least 54-44 with some races still counting). Historic
Democratic wins occurred in deep-red territory: Texas Senate (James
Talarico over Ken Paxton), Kansas (first Democratic senator in nearly a
century), South Carolina, Iowa, Ohio, Maine (Susan Collins defeated),
Michigan, and more. Governorships also shifted, including Texas. This
was a broad repudiation, not just swing districts.

Exit polls and post-election analyses align with parts of Vance’s
diagnosis while adding others. The Iran war ranked as a major or
crucial factor for a large share of voters (often 60%+ in various
polls), with majorities calling the decision to use force wrong. Cost of
living/inflation, tariffs’ effects on prices, energy costs tied to the
conflict, and broader dissatisfaction with Trump’s performance were also
dominant. Some Trump 2024 voters did switch; others stayed home or the
composition shifted in key groups (Hispanics, younger voters, non-college
Whites in places). Turnout was not catastrophic for Republicans in many
states, supporting Vance’s “our people showed up” point, but the
preference shift among prior supporters was decisive.

### The four reasons and the internal debate

Vance’s list tracks realist/America First critiques that predated the
midterms: skepticism of open-ended Middle East commitments, prioritization
of domestic costs over foreign entanglements, and questions about the
intensity of the U.S.-Israel relationship amid an expanded conflict. He
praises Rachel Campos-Duffy’s call for diplomacy as politically
sound. Trump has said the war was “a piece of it” (or similar)
but defended the decision and said he would do it again, framing it as
necessary against Iranian nuclear ambitions. This creates an early public
gap between the president and vice president on how to weigh the costs.

Broader commentary has mixed the war with tariffs, inflation persistence,
candidate selection in some races, and simple midterm-year fatigue
with the president’s party. Some analyses emphasize economic pain
more than foreign policy; others treat the war as the clearest “why
now” accelerator because it linked directly to higher fuel and living
costs. Vance is explicit that establishment talk of amnesty as the
solution is gaslighting—the data he cites does not point there.

### Where this leaves things

Vance frames the loss as honest data, not a turnout failure, and urges
channeling anger into electing Republicans who match the worldview of
those switchers (end the war, domestic focus, America First). He remains
hopeful about the country while telling “I told you so” critics they
were right. The post functions both as diagnosis and as positioning: it
distances him from the most unpopular elements of the administration’s
record while claiming fidelity to the 2024 coalition’s priorities.

The empirical core holds up against the reported results and polling:
Republican-leaning composition in places like Florida did not translate
into Republican victories at the scale needed, switchers existed and
cited those issues, and the war was highly salient. How much weight each
factor carried, whether the war was avoidable or worth the political
price, and what “change our relationship to Israel” should mean in
practice remain contested. Any durable Republican recovery will have
to address the concrete costs voters felt—prices, energy, and the
sense that foreign policy crowded out domestic priorities—rather than
treating the outcome as purely a messaging or turnout problem.
