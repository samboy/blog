In a Connecticut appellate ruling affirming summary judgment, Sacred Heart University prevailed against a former student’s breach-of-contract claim stemming from an immediate suspension.

The university’s student handbook formed an enforceable contract with students. It required treating them with respect, dignity, and compassion and cautioned against presuming guilt from allegations alone. At the same time, it expressly empowered the dean of students to impose an immediate suspension whenever necessary to protect the welfare and safety of the campus community, including cases involving serious criminal accusations. No prior internal investigation was required under the handbook’s terms.

That authority was exercised after police notified the dean of a sexual-assault report. Dhameer Bradley, then a student and football player, was barred from classes, university events, and team participation. On counsel’s advice he later withdrew, forfeiting his athletic scholarship. He eventually returned, completed his degree in December 2018, and graduated; the scholarship was never restored.

The underlying incident had occurred at an off-campus party in Bridgeport on 15 October 2016 involving Bradley, fellow student Malik St. Hilaire, and Nikki Yovino. After sexual intercourse in a bathroom, Yovino told friends she had been assaulted, sought hospital care the next morning, underwent a sexual-assault examination, and gave a police statement naming the two men. Yovino later recanted, pleaded guilty to falsely reporting an incident and interfering with an officer, yet continued to maintain the encounter was non-consensual—an issue the appellate court did not decide.

Bradley and St. Hilaire sued both Yovino and the university; the sole claim against the institution was breach of the handbook contract. Bradley contended the suspension, based solely on an uncorroborated police report and without university investigation, violated the promised presumption against guilt. The trial court disagreed, holding that the handbook’s suspension provisions controlled. The Court of Appeals concurred: the dean acted within the discretionary authority granted to safeguard the community, the handbook imposed no duty of prior investigation for serious criminal allegations, and summary judgment for the university was proper.
