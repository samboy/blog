Salon is ending its run after 31 years of independent digital journalism.

What began in November 1995 as an experiment by a small group of journalists on a still-new web has closed. Those founders set out to build a publication with a distinct voice, genuine curiosity, and the conviction that the internet could connect reporting and readers in fresh ways. Over the decades that followed, Salon mixed politics, culture, criticism, and ideas on its own terms—probing power, challenging assumptions, elevating overlooked stories, and helping shape conversations about the forces reshaping daily life. The work aimed less at chasing headlines than at making sense of them.

That history belongs to the people who created it: the founders who imagined the project, the successive waves of journalists, editors, photographers, producers, designers, and business staff who sustained it, the contributors who lent their voices, and the alumni who carried its approach outward. Readers formed the other half of the enterprise. They arrived for the reporting, essays, criticism, and unexpected finds; they shared, argued, subscribed, and returned, turning a website into a community where ideas carried weight.

The economics of independent digital publishing shifted dramatically over those years. After weighing the options, the organization concluded its model was no longer viable. The decision closes a long chapter, yet the lasting measure is what filled those years—the journalism produced, the perspectives aired, the conversations sparked, and the community that gathered around them. Salon showed one path digital journalism could take; that demonstration is what remains.
