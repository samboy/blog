International refusals of Brazilian extradition requests highlight
concerns over political prosecutions under Justice Alexandre de Moraes.

Multiple countries and institutions—including Spain, Italy, INTERPOL,
and the United States during the Biden administration—have rejected
extradition demands from Brazil targeting individuals accused by
Moraes. These denials rest on findings that the underlying cases appear
politically driven or compromised by procedural irregularities.

A recent example involves Italy’s refusal to extradite a former senior
aide to Moraes known online as @edutagli. Brazilian authorities sought his
return after accusing him of serving as a source for a major 2024 leak
of materials from Moraes’s office. That leak supported roughly twenty
investigative articles published with Folha that documented patterns
of alleged overreach against political opponents. Italian authorities
determined the requested proceedings would not meet extradition standards,
citing Moraes’s simultaneous roles as purported victim, investigator,
and adjudicator.

The pattern extends beyond any single case. Dozens of Brazilian
journalists, activists, bloggers, and politicians have left the country,
citing the risk of imprisonment for political opposition. Flavio Bolsonaro
is among those who departed amid what critics describe as targeted
pressure from Moraes and aligned elements of the Supreme Court. In this
environment, opponents of Moraes view remaining in Brazil as untenable
once arrest appears likely, given the perception that outcomes are
predetermined and unconstrained by ordinary legal limits.

International asylum protections exist precisely for situations in
which a domestic justice system is seen as subordinated to political
ends. Supporters of the exiles draw parallels to other high-profile cases
in which political figures, including Brazil’s own Lula, have previously
endorsed such safeguards. The cumulative effect of these extradition
refusals and departures has become one of the more visible international
critiques of judicial practice in Brazil over the past several years.
