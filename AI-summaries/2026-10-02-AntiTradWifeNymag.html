Historians note that this vision clashed with earlier Enlightenment ideas separating public reform from private affection; instead it encouraged retreat into self-contained household units. Figures like Phyllis Schlafly argued that a woman could find achievement, financial safety, and emotional fulfillment simply by bearing children and remaining at home. Critics later observed that the same rhetoric justified reducing women’s public roles and dismantling broader social bonds, leaving dignity and safety contingent on loyalty inside the household “bunker.”
