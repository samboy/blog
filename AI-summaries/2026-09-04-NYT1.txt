The text argues that Gloria Steinem’s death is especially bittersweet because it comes at a time when many of the gains associated with second-wave feminism appear to be under threat. Steinem’s life once seemed like a story of steady feminist progress—from a period when abortion was illegal and women faced widespread discrimination to a 2016 moment when feminism appeared culturally and politically ascendant. The author suggests that the subsequent backlash makes Steinem’s legacy both more painful and more relevant.

### Main points

Steinem represented major feminist progress.
Early in her career, women faced severe legal, professional, and social barriers. Steinem helped challenge those norms through journalism, activism, and organizing.

The optimism of 2016 has given way to a feminist backlash.
The author points to restrictions on abortion, attacks on women’s rights, reduced parental benefits, and rhetoric portraying women’s economic and social power as a problem.

Steinem’s legacy matters precisely because progress is reversible.
She did not eliminate sexism, but she helped make overt misogyny less socially acceptable. The author’s central lesson is essentially: if previous generations changed society, today’s generation can do it again.

Human connection and organizing were central to Steinem’s feminism.
She valued consciousness-raising groups, feminist gatherings, and face-to-face relationships. She believed political movements needed genuine communities, not just shared ideas.

The internet and social-media algorithms have made organizing harder.
The author connects today’s polarization and anti-feminist backlash to online systems that reward outrage, conflict, tribalism, and constant escalation.

Social media has harmed feminism from both inside and outside.
Online feminism has sometimes fractured through purity tests and internal conflict, while the “manosphere” has grown by exploiting resentment and misogyny.

The proposed answer is to rebuild real-world communities.
The author highlights organizations that combine politics with social activities—sports, comedy, gatherings—as examples of how movements can combat loneliness and isolation.

Steinem herself envisioned this kind of network.
She imagined a nationwide “honeycomb” of small groups that would blend the personal and political, modeled partly on Alcoholics Anonymous.

### Central takeaway

The article is ultimately less an obituary than a call to action: Steinem’s greatest lesson may be that lasting social change requires people to organize together in the real world. In an era dominated by isolating, outrage-driven algorithms, rebuilding strong human networks could be essential to renewing feminism and other progressive movements.
