Most young adults lead modest sex lives. Surveys of 18- to 24-year-olds show that the single most common number of partners in the past year is one, followed closely by zero. Roughly two-thirds of young men and four-fifths of young women report falling into that narrow range. Only a smaller slice—about 22 percent of men and 12 percent of women—report three or more partners, and the evidence indicates those more active individuals largely pair with one another rather than distributing encounters across the broader population.

This pattern is not new. Data collected by sociologist Mark Regnerus from more than 15,000 American adults already revealed a similar concentration: roughly 20 percent of men ages 25–50 accounted for about 70 percent of reported sexual partnerships with women, while a comparable share of women accounted for roughly 65 percent of reported male partners. The overlapping minorities, not a one-sided extraction of partners by a male elite, explain the bulk of higher partner counts. Later General Social Survey analyses confirm the same distribution among younger cohorts and show that the share of young men reporting three or more partners has stayed remarkably stable since at least 2000.

Online narratives often invert this picture. A widespread story, especially among young men, claims that a tiny group of highly desirable men monopolize sexual access while everyone else is shut out—creating a winner-take-all “chadopoly” and leaving a permanent underclass of frustrated outsiders. That framing fuels resentment and treats dating as a zero-sum contest. Yet the numbers undermine the claim. High partner counts remain confined to statistical minorities of both sexes; most sex still occurs inside relationships; and the pool of women open to casual, uncommitted encounters is itself limited and heavily pursued. Even the men who succeed with multiple partners experience frequent rejection while competing for that small group.

Public conversation tends to lurch between opposite extremes—either that young people have largely stopped having sex, or that they are more promiscuous than any prior generation. Reality sits in the middle. Sexlessness has risen, yet a small, highly active subset now finds it easier to locate one another through dating apps, and reduced stigma has made their activity more visible. That visibility, amplified online, creates the misleading impression of a sexual free-for-all that the data simply do not support.
