In late September 2025, Tompkins County District Attorney Matthew Van Houten announced that his office would reopen a criminal inquiry into events at Cornell University’s Chi Phi fraternity house nearly a year earlier, planning to present the matter to a grand jury once charges could be properly framed. The move came amid a newly filed civil lawsuit and renewed public attention to an incident that campus authorities had already investigated.

On the night of 19 October 2024, according to the 101-page complaint filed by a woman identified only as Jane Doe, the then-20-year-old Cornell undergraduate left her sorority after drinking and walked alone to the Chi Phi house in Ithaca to meet a male friend. She alleges that seven men from the chapter—later dubbed the “Cornell Seven”—sexually assaulted her for hours while she was intoxicated and incapable of consent, involving both alcohol and ketamine. The suit names the university, the fraternity, and the individual men, accusing them of sexual assault and battery, negligence, breach of contract, and violations of New York’s Human Rights Law. It also includes an image from a fraternity group chat in which participants discussed, in coarse terms, the prospect of a sexual encounter with her shortly before 2 a.m. on 20 October.

Two days after the alleged assault, text messages exchanged between one of the defendants and a woman matching Doe’s description show the man apologizing for “how things went down,” citing fuzzy memories and regret that he and another participant had been too intoxicated to intervene. The woman replied that her own recollection was “super clouded,” that everyone had been intoxicated, that “none of the sexual stuff was illegal,” that she had liked being with him and another man, and that they might smoke together again—provided the extra men and ketamine were left out.

Doe reported the matter to Cornell University Police on 8 November 2024. The university quickly suspended the local Chi Phi chapter and a number of students, opened both a campus police investigation and a Title IX civil-rights inquiry, and later referred the case to a panel of faculty and staff. That panel imposed a range of sanctions that included expulsions and suspensions; Cornell has stated that while essays formed part of some disciplinary outcomes, they were never the sole consequence. The Xi Chapter itself was closed and remains barred from campus. University officials have rejected as false the claim that they failed to investigate or punish those involved, describing the events as “appalling then and remain so now.”

When Van Houten’s office first reviewed the case, it concluded that Doe’s November 2024 statement to campus police did not meet the legal threshold for charges. In that account, according to the district attorney, she described her participation in drug use and sexual activity as voluntary, conscious, and consensual. Subsequent interview transcripts obtained by reporters, however, show her stating of the later portion of the night, “I can say with 100% confidence I was raped.” Van Houten has said he had not previously seen the full transcripts and has since contacted Doe and her lawyers for additional information ahead of the grand-jury presentation.

The civil complaint and the university newspaper’s decision to publish the defendants’ names have intensified scrutiny, but the criminal process now centers on whether the evidence, viewed afresh, supports formal charges.
