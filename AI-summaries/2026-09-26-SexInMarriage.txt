Religious couples who regularly attend worship services stand out as an exception amid a broader American decline in sexual frequency. While married adults in their 20s to 50s once reported weekly sex at similar rates regardless of faith, secular pairs (those who rarely or never attend) have seen that share drop sharply. Regular churchgoers, by contrast, have largely held steady, and both husbands and wives in this group also report higher sexual satisfaction and greater overall marital happiness than their non-religious peers.

The pattern runs counter to familiar cultural portrayals of religious people as inhibited or dutiful rather than fulfilled. Shared faith appears to help by framing marriage and sex as sacred, encouraging virtues such as forgiveness and fidelity, and fostering practices like joint prayer that ease daily stresses and strengthen emotional closeness. Couples who attend together tend to be more aligned in their beliefs, which supports relational harmony; many also approach differences in desire with a practical generosity—husbands respecting moments of disinterest, wives responding to initiation—rather than treating the typical gap as a source of resentment.

Secular couples face additional headwinds. Negative portrayals of men and marriage that circulate heavily online may heighten anxiety, especially among women, while the broader erosion of neighborhood ties, friendships, and community life leaves many without the external encouragement that religious networks still supply through worship, small groups, and shared norms. In an era marked by isolation, faith communities continue to provide one of the more consistent forms of real-world support for intimate partnership.
