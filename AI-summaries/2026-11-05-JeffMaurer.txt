Moderate Democratic candidates substantially outperformed leftist ones
in the midterms, according to the metrics the author established months
earlier.

Democratic Senate candidates as a group posted a strong average swing
of 16.2 points relative to Kamala Harris’s 2024 results in their
states. This undercuts claims that the electorate contains no meaningful
swing voters. When the same races are examined against other statewide
contests (where data were available), the pattern holds: more mainstream
Democrats generally ran ahead of or closer to their co-partisans than
progressive ones did.

The clearest divide appears among candidates endorsed by Bernie Sanders,
used here as a practical proxy for the left. In competitive races, those
candidates ranked among the weakest relative performers nationwide. None
matched the relative strength shown by typical Democratic gubernatorial
nominees in the same states. Charts isolating the Sanders-backed
contenders (and excluding non-competitive races for clarity) show them
lagging the field on both the presidential-benchmark and the intra-state
comparisons. A few individual notes stand out in the data—James
Talarico’s strong showing against a damaged opponent, and weaker results
for figures such as Jeff Merkley—but the aggregate signal is consistent.

The author treats these results as confirmation of a long-standing finding
in political science rather than a surprise: far-left positioning is
broadly unpopular with the broader electorate. The practical takeaway
offered is that Democrats can capitalize on Republican unpopularity
by emphasizing results over ideological purity, and that seeking the
endorsement of Sanders is unlikely to help. The midterm numbers, in this
reading, give voters’ preferences unusual clarity.
