Text adventures

November 24, 2026

Today, I discuss old school text adventure games.

Text Adventures

Back when a “LLM” was nothing more than a weird mistyping of “LLC”, a lot of home computers, such as the TRS-80, had for all intents and purposes no graphics. People still wanted to play video games on the TRS-80, so one Scott Adams (not the recently deceased guy who made the Dilbert comics) figured out how to make a type of game called a “text adventure” small enough to fit on a TRS-80.

A text adventure game (or “interactive fiction” game, to use a more recent term for this kind of video game) is a game where the computer uses text to describe where the hero is, and then the hero types in simple one word or two word commands to interact with the world. These games were on very primitive computers with limited memory, so they could not understand natural English. Instead, commands were in the form of “GET BANANA” or “GO POOL” (where “GO POOL” was short for “go in to the pool”).

In order to minimize the memory used by this game, Scott Adams’s text adventures uses a special data format and an interpreter to run adventure games. Well over two dozen text adventures were eventually made in this format, by both Scott Adams and other developers (such as Brian Howarth). By 1986, text adventures were no longer as popular, and Scott Adams’s company selling these games went out of business.

The problem these adventures had was two-fold:

  • Since computers were not at the time capable of any kind of creativity, a person writing the text adventure had to come up with some response to pretty much anything someone typed in. If you typed in something the author wasn’t expecting, the program couldn’t give you a response better than “I don’t understand you” or “I can’t do that”. LLMs fixed this issue many years later.

  • Since the adventures were very tiny (my own Desert Adventure, for example, has under 1300 words of text for the entire game), the only way to keep them interesting for more than a couple of minutes was by adding puzzles to the game. Pretty soon, to keep the game viable, Scott Adams had to add some really nasty nay to impossible puzzles to later releases of his games.

One problem with making difficult puzzles was that they often required frustrating “guess the verb” grinding to solve a puzzle. I will give a real world example.

Guess the verb

A long time ago, I spent weeks trying to solve “Ghost Town”, and while I was able to get most treasures, I could never complete the game.

Before continuing, I need to post a warning: A spoiler for this really old text adventure follows.

One of the puzzles is this: You have a horse in the game which carries you to a distant place and then rides off, leaving the player stranded. But, one can summon an Indian ghost.

Once the ghost is summoned, it’s obvious that one has to ask the ghost help with getting back home. But, to do so with the ghost is an exercise in frustration. “TALK GHOST” doesn’t work because Ghost Town doesn’t have the verb “TALK”. “ASK GHOST” also doesn’t work—the game doesn’t have the verb “ASK” either. I was never able to figure out how to ask the ghost to take me home again, until many years later I went through the game’s data file and discovered the combination was “SAY HOW” (“SAY HELP” or “SAY HOME” or any other word after “SAY” doesn’t work).

With a modern LLM, one would just say “Tell the ghost I need help getting back to town” then the LLM would have the ghost take the hero back home.

Another person felt that a different puzzle was unfair; I actually found that puzzle easy (if silly), but, to be fair, unless one is familiar with the board game Monopoly), the puzzle won’t make any sense.

Playing these games

I have made a Git repo of an updated version of the interpreter used to play these old Scott Adams style games, in addition to files for playing adventure games online. Back in 2007, I made a new open source Scott Adams game, which is included with this repo. This game is small and it’s pretty easy, but it’s a good introduction to these old text adventure games.

While the online reviews felt the game was too easy, it really isn’t: One buddy who play tested this game for me was only able to solve half of it before getting stuck, and someone else sent me an email complaining the final puzzle was too difficult to solve.

One can play this game online; people using smartphones should play the small screen version of this game.
